# 🏦 Retail Bank Customer Targeting

> Finding the customers worth calling, in SQL, where every name on the list carries the reason it is there.

A marketing team asks for a list of customers to contact. The easy answer is
to sort by balance and take the top 500. This project is the harder answer:
a SQL pipeline that scores customers on behavior, assigns each one a reason
code, and produces a ranked call list that can be audited row by row.

Runs on DuckDB with no setup. The dataset is synthetic and generated by the
first script, so the whole thing is reproducible from a clone.

---

## ▶️ Run it

```bash
pip install duckdb
duckdb bank.duckdb < sql/01_schema.sql
duckdb bank.duckdb < sql/02_customer_features.sql
duckdb bank.duckdb < sql/03_segments.sql
duckdb bank.duckdb < sql/04_target_list.sql
```

## 🧠 The idea

Most customer targeting fails in one of two ways. Either it ranks on a level
(balance, revenue) and produces a list of people who were already fine, or it
produces a list with no explanation, which is useless to whoever has to make
the call.

Two decisions shape this pipeline.

**Direction beats level.** Every feature is calculated over two windows: the
90 days before the as-of date, and the 90 days before that. A customer with a
large balance and falling activity is a different problem from a customer with
the same balance and rising activity, and only the comparison tells you which
one you have.

**Every row carries a reason.** The output is not a score alone. Each
targeted customer is attached to one of five reason codes, and the reason
determines the script the caller uses:

| Reason | Trigger | Urgency |
|---|---|---|
| `ATTRITION_RISK` | Activity down against the prior quarter on an active relationship | 1.00 |
| `SALARY_LOST` | Recurring salary credits stopped this quarter | 0.95 |
| `REACTIVATE` | No transactions in 90+ days, balance still held | 0.70 |
| `IDLE_CASH` | High checking balance, no savings product | 0.55 |
| `CROSS_SELL` | Rising activity or inflows on a single-product relationship | 0.45 |

A customer who qualifies under several reasons is kept once, under the most
urgent one. Priority is 60% relationship value and 40% reason urgency, with
value capped so that one large depositor cannot own the top of the list.

## 📊 What it produces

Segments across 2,000 customers:

| Growing | 785 | $28,866 | $22.66M |
| Stable | 570 | $26,847 | $15.30M |
| Cooling | 493 | $18,512 | $9.13M |

The call list narrows 2,000 customers to 986, covering $22.09M in deposits:

| Reason | Customers | Deposits at stake | Avg score |
|---|---|---|---|
| `IDLE_CASH` | 383 | $9.48M | 41.8 |
| `ATTRITION_RISK` | 257 | $8.60M | 66.6 |

| `REACTIVATE` | 64 | $1.01M | 40.6 |

## ✅ How I know the rules work

The data generator assigns every customer a hidden behavior profile
(`stable`, `growing`, `attriting`, `dormant`) and writes it to a column that
none of the feature, segment, or targeting queries are allowed to read. It is
opened once, at the end of `04`, to check whether the reason codes actually
found what they claim to find.

| Reason | Attriting | Dormant | Growing | Stable |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `REACTIVATE` | 0% | **100%** | 0% | 0% |
| `SALARY_LOST` | 30% | **64%** | 3% | 3% |
| `ATTRITION_RISK` | **49%** | 11% | 11% | 29% |
| `IDLE_CASH` | 0% | 0% | **60%** | 40% |
| `CROSS_SELL` | 24% | 0% | 28% | **48%** |

Against a base rate of 27% attriting and 10% dormant in the population.

**The honest read:** three of the five rules work. `REACTIVATE` is exact.
`SALARY_LOST` and `ATTRITION_RISK` both lift well above base rate, and
`ATTRITION_RISK` catching some dormant customers is fine, since those were
worth a call anyway.

`CROSS_SELL` does not work. At 48% stable and 24% attriting it is barely
distinguishable from picking single-product customers at random, which is
roughly what it does. Rising activity on one product turns out to be a weak
signal on its own. It stays in the repo as written rather than being quietly
tuned, because a rule that fails validation is the most useful thing this
check produces.

---

## ⚠️ What this is not

The data is synthetic and the behavior profiles are generated, so the
validation above tests whether the SQL finds the patterns that were planted,
not whether these rules would hold on real customers. Real thresholds would
come from outcome data: who actually left, who actually took the product.

The scoring weights are asserted, not learned. On real data the next step is
to replace the urgency constants with fitted uplift estimates, at which point
this becomes the feature layer under a model rather than the whole answer.
